One Dashboard. Your Entire Financial Picture.

OripioFin is a modern financial management platform designed to bring multiple areas of personal and business finance into one centralized dashboard.

The experience combines:

  • Wallet management
  • Multi-currency accounts
  • Savings
  • Investment tracking
  • Cash-flow monitoring
  • Transactions
  • Invoices
  • Recurring payments
  • Subscriptions
  • Financial analytics

into a single financial workspace.

Instead of forcing users to move between multiple banking apps, spreadsheets, investment platforms, and payment tools, OripioFin creates one consolidated view of their financial activity.

The core product idea is simple:

Understand where your money is, where it is going, and how it is growing — from one place.

Why OripioFin Exists

Modern users rarely manage their entire financial life through a single account.

A user may have:

  • Money in a bank account
  • Savings in another account
  • Investments in a separate platform
  • Multiple currencies
  • Recurring subscriptions
  • Business invoices
  • Incoming and outgoing payments

As financial activity grows, understanding the complete picture becomes increasingly difficult.

The problem is not necessarily a lack of financial data.

The problem is too much data spread across too many places.

OripioFin brings these different financial activities together so users can understand their overall financial position without switching between multiple systems.

The Business Problem

The primary business problem is financial fragmentation.

Users have access to a lot of financial information, but that information is often disconnected.

This creates several challenges.

01 — Fragmented Financial Data

Wallets, savings, investments, transactions, and invoices may exist in separate systems.

Users have to manually switch between platforms to understand their financial situation.

02 — Difficult Financial Monitoring

A balance alone does not tell the complete story.

Users also need to understand:

  • Cash flow
  • Spending
  • Savings growth
  • Investment performance
  • Upcoming payments
  • Recurring expenses

Without a consolidated overview, financial decision-making becomes harder.

03 — Multi-Currency Complexity

International users may manage USD, EUR, GBP, BDT, and other currencies simultaneously.

This creates additional cognitive load.

Users need to know:

How much money do I have?

Where is it?

In which currency?

What is active or inactive?

04 — Important Information Gets Buried

Transactions and financial activities can quickly become a long list.

Without hierarchy, users may struggle to identify what requires attention.

05 — Financial Management Can Feel Overwhelming

Financial dashboards often contain charts, numbers, tables, filters, and account information.

The challenge is presenting all of this without making the interface feel intimidating.

Primary Audience — Financially Active Professionals

People who manage multiple financial accounts and want a centralized view of their money.

They may have:

  • Multiple accounts
  • Savings
  • Investments
  • Recurring expenses
  • Multiple currencies

Freelancers & Remote Workers

Freelancers who receive payments internationally may need to manage currencies such as:

  • USD
  • EUR
  • GBP
  • BDT

They may also need to track invoices, subscriptions, and recurring business expenses.

For this audience, a centralized financial dashboard can reduce the need to manage information manually across multiple tools.

Small Business Owners

Business owners need visibility into:

  • Cash flow
  • Transactions
  • Invoices
  • Recurring expenses
  • Payments
  • Financial performance

OripioFin's combination of financial overview and operational finance features supports this use case.

International Users

Users dealing with multiple currencies need a simple way to understand balances across different financial accounts.

The multi-currency wallet is particularly relevant for this audience.

01 — Too Many Financial Tools

Users often need separate platforms for:

Banking + Savings + Investments + Payments + Invoices + Subscriptions

This creates unnecessary friction.

02 — No Single Source of Truth

Users may know individual account balances but still struggle to understand their overall financial position.

03 — Multi-Currency Confusion

When money exists in different currencies, tracking balances becomes more complicated.

04 — Cash Flow Is Difficult to Understand

A user can have a healthy account balance while still experiencing poor cash flow.

Understanding inflow and outflow is therefore essential.

05 — Important Transactions Get Lost

Large transaction histories make it difficult to identify important activity without search and filtering.

06 — Financial Analytics Can Feel Complex

Charts and financial data need to be understandable at a glance.

Users should not need to be financial analysts to understand their dashboard.

The product experience focused on six key objectives:

1. Create one centralized financial overview

2. Make multi-currency management easier

3. Surface the most important financial information first

4. Turn complex financial data into understandable visuals

5. Make transactions and activities easy to find

6. Create a scalable financial workspace for different needs

01 — Start With an Overview

The dashboard begins with:

Overview

and:

Here is the summary of overall data

This is an important UX decision.

Instead of immediately showing detailed transactions, the interface starts with the user's financial summary.

The user first gets the answer to:

“How am I doing financially?”

Then they can explore individual areas.

Financial Overview Cards

The top section contains three major financial cards:

My Balance

Each card communicates:

  • Account type
  • Current value
  • Performance change
  • Short description
  • Next action

This creates an immediate financial snapshot.

Why the Card-Based Overview Works

Financial information contains many numbers.

Showing every number at the same visual weight would overwhelm the user.

Instead, the dashboard establishes a hierarchy:

Primary financial state

↓

Secondary financial accounts

↓

Detailed wallet data

↓

Cash flow

↓

Recent transactions

This progressive disclosure keeps the interface manageable.

My Balance

The My Balance card receives the strongest visual treatment.

It uses the primary brand color and contains the largest visual emphasis.

This communicates that the user's available balance is one of the most important pieces of information.

The card also provides:

+1.5%

as a performance indicator and:

See details

as the next action.

This creates a compact combination of:

Status + Change + Action

Savings & Investment

The Savings Account and Investment Portfolio cards are visually lighter.

This creates a clear hierarchy.

The user can quickly distinguish:

Available / Wallet Balance

from:

Savings

and:

Investments

A wallet balance is immediately accessible.

Savings represent reserved capital.

Investments represent long-term financial assets.

Multi-Currency Wallet

One of the strongest product features shown in the dashboard is:

My Wallet

The interface displays multiple currency accounts:

  • USD
  • EUR
  • BDT
  • GBP

Each account contains:

  • Currency
  • Balance
  • Monthly limit
  • Active/inactive status
  • More actions

This makes the wallet understandable without requiring the user to open each account individually.

Multi-Currency UX

The multi-currency cards solve an important problem:

Users need to understand multiple balances without mentally converting everything into one account.

Each currency gets its own visual identity through:

  • Currency symbol
  • Country flag
  • Balance
  • Limit
  • Status

This creates a simple mental model:

Currency → Balance → Limit → Status

Active & Inactive States

The wallet cards clearly communicate account status.

For example:

Active

appears as a positive status.

Inactive

appears differently for the GBP account.

This is a small but important UX decision.

Financial dashboards need to make account states obvious because inactive accounts can otherwise be mistaken for usable balances.

Cash Flow Analytics

The right side of the dashboard contains a large:

The chart provides a visual representation of:

Why Cash Flow Gets a Large Visualization

A balance tells the user:

How much do I have?

Cash flow tells the user:

How is my money moving?

These are fundamentally different questions.

By placing cash flow next to the wallet section, the dashboard lets users compare:

Current financial state

with:

Financial movement over time

Interactive Data Exploration

The chart includes:

Monthly / Yearly

controls.

This allows the user to change the time scale without leaving the dashboard.

A hover interaction also reveals detailed information:

July 23, 2026

Cashflow — $33,847.00

Inflow — -$7,456.00

This is an effective example of progressive disclosure.

The chart remains visually clean until the user needs more detail.

Recent Activities

The bottom section contains:

with a structured transaction table.

The table includes:

  • Activity
  • Order ID
  • Date
  • Time
  • Price
  • Status

This creates a bridge between high-level analytics and individual transactions.

The user can start with:

Overview

and progressively drill down into:

Specific financial activity

Search & Filter

The Recent Activities section includes:

Search

and:

Filter

controls.

This is important because transaction lists can grow quickly.

Rather than forcing users to scroll through every transaction, the interface gives them tools to find the information they need.

Financial Information Hierarchy

The dashboard follows a clear hierarchy:

Level 01 — Overall Financial Health

Balance, savings, investment.

Level 02 — Financial Accounts

Multi-currency wallet.

Level 03 — Financial Movement

Cash-flow chart.

Level 04 — Individual Activity

Recent transactions.

This is one of the strongest UX decisions in the design.

The dashboard contains many potential destinations.

Without a persistent navigation structure, users would have to rely on repeated back-and-forth interactions.

The sidebar provides constant access to:

  • Analytics
  • Transactions
  • Invoices
  • Recurring
  • Subscriptions
  • Settings

This makes the platform feel like a complete financial operating system rather than a single dashboard screen.

Analytics as a Separate Destination

The sidebar includes:

Analytics

This suggests that detailed financial analysis is available beyond the dashboard.

This creates a useful separation:

Dashboard

Visual Design Strategy

The visual design uses a clean fintech aesthetic.

Key characteristics include:

  • White surfaces
  • Deep green primary color
  • Soft grey borders
  • Rounded cards
  • Large financial numbers
  • Minimal iconography
  • Subtle gradients
  • Light shadows
  • Spacious layout
  • High information density without visual clutter

The green brand color is particularly effective for financial products because it naturally connects with:

  • Money
  • Growth
  • Positive performance
  • Financial confidence

Green as a Functional Color

The green color is not used everywhere.

It is reserved for important financial states and actions.

For example:

  • Primary balance card
  • Positive performance
  • Active states
  • Primary CTA
  • Upgrade action

This gives green functional meaning.

Users begin to associate it with:

Positive / Active / Important

rather than simply treating it as decoration.

Number-First Design

Financial dashboards are fundamentally number-driven.

The interface therefore uses large typography for:

  • Balance
  • Savings
  • Investments
  • Cash flow
  • Currency balances

This allows users to scan the dashboard quickly.

The user doesn't need to read every label to understand the overall state.

Progressive Disclosure

The dashboard follows a clear principle:

Show the summary first. Reveal the details when needed.

For example:

Summary

$20,520.32

↓

Detail

See details

Similarly:

Cash Flow

$342,323.44

↓

Chart

Monthly activity

↓

Tooltip

Exact date + cash flow + inflow

This prevents information overload.

What I Focused on as a Product Designer

The biggest challenge was balancing high information density with simplicity.

I focused on:

Financial Hierarchy

Making the most important financial information immediately visible.

Multi-Currency Clarity

Giving every currency a clear balance, limit, and status.

Data Visualization

Turning cash-flow data into something users can understand visually.

Progressive Disclosure

Showing details only when users need them.

Scalable Navigation

Creating a structure that can support more financial features over time.

Actionability

Making every major information block lead to a logical next action.

The final dashboard creates a centralized financial command center.

A user can understand their financial state within seconds:

How much do I have?

How much have I saved?

How much have I invested?

What currencies do I hold?

How is my cash flow performing?

What happened recently?

And when they need more detail, they can move deeper into:

Analytics → Transactions → Invoices → Recurring → Subscriptions

The result is a product experience that turns complex financial information into a structured and scannable workspace.

The Core UX Journey

The dashboard follows:

Overview → Understand → Explore → Analyze → Act

Overview

See the most important numbers.

Understand

Review balances and account status.

Explore

Check wallet and cash-flow information.

Analyze

Use analytics and historical data.

Act

Manage transactions, invoices, recurring payments, and subscriptions.

Key UX Decisions

From Multiple Financial Tools → One Workspace

The product combines different financial activities into one centralized interface.

From Raw Numbers → Financial Hierarchy

Instead of presenting every number equally, the interface prioritizes:

Balance → Savings → Investments → Wallet → Cash Flow → Transactions

From Multi-Currency Complexity → Clear Account Cards

Each currency has a consistent card structure:

Currency + Balance + Limit + Status

From Static Data → Interactive Analytics

The cash-flow chart provides:

Time range + Visual trend + Interactive detail

without cluttering the default view.

From Long Transaction Lists → Searchable Activity

Search and filtering make large amounts of transaction data easier to navigate.

From Dashboard Overload → Progressive Disclosure

The dashboard communicates the summary first and allows users to drill down when necessary.

Key Takeaways

A financial dashboard should answer the biggest questions first.

Users should understand their overall financial position before seeing detailed data.

Multi-currency products need strong visual consistency.

Users should be able to compare currencies without learning a different interface for each one.

Charts should support decisions, not decoration.

The cash-flow visualization exists to help users understand movement over time.

Financial data needs hierarchy.

Balance, savings, investments, wallet, cash flow, and transactions should not compete equally for attention.

Search and filtering become essential as data grows.

A good financial dashboard should work just as well with thousands of transactions as it does with a few.

Progressive disclosure keeps complex products approachable.

Users get the information they need without being overwhelmed by everything the system knows.
